G.01 / FRONTIERResearch Proposal

What does survival cost?

Unified resources as an interaction experiment.

Rifts have opened and something is coming through. In Frontier, every shot, dash and ability you use costs the same health that keeps you alive. It’s a third-person action game built as an experiment, to find out whether tying survival to every move changes the way people fight.

PROJECT NOTES

The Research Question

  • Most action games keep health and abilities on separate meters, so using a skill rarely puts you in danger. Frontier asks what happens when both come out of the same pool, in a game with a lot of fast movement.
  • Does sharing one pool make players more aggressive, or more careful?
  • Does it change how often they choose melee over ranged attacks?
  • Do lasting wounds and getting health back from kills change how players judge risk over a whole mission?

The Proposed System

  • Shared Pool — every attack and ability spends the same resource that keeps you alive.
  • Wounds — damage leaves wounds that slow regeneration. Bad wounds can stop it completely or start draining you.
  • Kill Recovery — defeating enemies gives resource back, so the design nudges players to keep fighting instead of hiding.
  • Custom Builds — eight slots, four main and four sub, filled with weapons and abilities from the modular Trigger system, so players are free to put together their own build.
  • Movement — a set of movement abilities for getting in and out of fights fast, since mobility is part of what the study looks at.
  • Setting — a near-future world where rifts let alien swarms through. Missions have you holding invasion zones, rescuing civilians and destroying enemy structures, which gives the tests plenty of variety.

How It Will Be Tested

  • Playtests — around 15 to 30 players who already play action or shooter games.
  • Comparison — the same missions with the shared pool, and possibly a version with separate meters as a baseline.
  • Gameplay Data — ability use, how resources are spent and recovered, survival time, mission completion, and the mix of melee and ranged attacks.
  • Player Feedback — surveys on how tense it felt, what strategies people used, and whether the system made sense to them.
  • Analysis — comparing aggressive and defensive play, linking resource use to survival, and grouping players by the strategies they settle into.

Technical Plan

  • Unreal Engine 5, using a mix of C++ and Blueprints.
  • Core systems: resource management for regeneration, wounds and decay, the Trigger ability framework, behavior-tree enemy AI, and mission scripting.
  • Project D was the groundwork prototype for this, testing a shared health-and-ammo pool with wounds and kill recovery in a smaller first-person shooter.
  • Balance is the hardest part. The pool needs to create tension without making players afraid to use their abilities.
  • This is still a proposal. Nothing has been playtested yet, so no results are claimed.
G.02 / FEATURED PROJECTVR Team Project

Patchwork Arsenal

Every gun is a stranger. Learn it, lose it, repeat.

A VR roguelike FPS set inside a fractured simulation built to test experimental weapons. Every run hands you a gun assembled from parts it rolled seconds ago. You will learn its recoil, its reload, its weight, and then the dungeon takes it back and gives you another. Go deep enough and the simulation stops pretending to be stable.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • In VR a resource the player can’t read at a glance isn’t a resource, and an event without feedback can’t be felt.
  • Health, currency, ammo and a freshly rolled gun’s stats all had to fit on a wristband glanced at mid-fight.
  • There’s no screen to flash red when you’re hit, so sound and haptics carry the damage. Feedback is load-bearing here, not polish.

Weapons and Gameplay

  • Procedural pistols — damage, fire rate, magazine, recoil and bullet speed rolled independently across four rarity tiers, so no two guns handle alike.
  • Modular assembly — a shared lower receiver plus a barrel guard pulled from a parts pool, giving every generated weapon its own silhouette.
  • Recoil — kicks the gun and recovers over time, with the shot resolved before the kick so aim isn’t punished by its own recoil.
  • Physical reload — eject the magazine, take a fresh one from the ammo pouch, seat it in the well and pull the slide. Magazines and muzzle flash are recycled rather than respawned.

Player Systems and Feedback

  • Health and currency — driving a state machine that handles the world logic.
  • Wrist HUD — showing a segmented health bar that reads partial damage, live currency and ammo, and the stats of the gun in hand.
  • Health bars — over every enemy, which also became the entry point the support enemy heals its allies through.
  • The economy loop — gems burst from a kill, and a shop holds items as previews until the purchase clears, with rarity scaling as the dungeon deepens.
  • Feedback service — positional sound, non-repeating clips, per-hand haptics, and hit confirmation that pitches up with the damage dealt.
  • Progression — survives quitting, including the tutorial unlock that gates the start button.

Design Takeaway

  • Combinatorial complexity only pays off when the player can read the choice and feel its consequence.
  • The feedback layer isn’t decoration on a system. It’s how the system becomes playable.
  • Owning a system isn’t the same as owning the outcome. Losing part of the weapon generator cost less than defending it would have, and what shipped was a more polished generator rather than many complex components.
G.03 / GAMEPLAYCourse Prototype

Project D[ungeon]

Every shot costs you survivability.

Deep under an old dwarven city, something has been waiting for you. Your gun doesn’t use bullets, it uses your health, so every shot leaves you a little closer to dying. Push through the halls, caves and ruins, keep killing to stay alive, and hope you still have enough left when you reach the boss.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • Almost all shooter games have health and ammo as two separate resources. We wanted to merge them, so that shooting actually costs you health and survivability.
  • The hard part was making it feel fair. If shooting hurts too much, players stop shooting. If health comes back too easily, the idea doesn’t matter much anymore.
  • Players also need to see how much they have left at a glance, even while they’re running around and dodging enemies.

The Resource System

  • Shared Pool — health and ammo are the same number, so every shot comes out of your health.
  • Two Kinds of Loss — health spent on shooting comes back on its own, but damage from enemies only partly recovers.
  • Kill Rewards — each kill gives back a little health, so fighting is how you get yourself out of trouble.
  • Wounds — taking too many hits slows your regeneration down, and eventually starts deducting health, until you find a health pack.

Combat and Enemies

  • Weapons — an assault rifle and an SMG, each with rarity tiers and its own stats. Players can pick them up and switch between weapon slots.
  • Weapon Feel — equip, fire, reload and holster animations, with sounds and effects to match.
  • Weapon Display — shows which gun you’re holding and its rarity.
  • Enemies — a brawler that charges at you, and a ranged enemy that throws molotovs from a distance.
  • Boss — has weak spots on its jaw and shell, and spawns smaller larvae during the fight so you can’t just focus on one target.
  • Enemy Spawning — fills each room with enemies as you move deeper into the dungeon.

Design Takeaway

  • Merging health and ammo ended up shaping most of the game on its own, like how fast people play, how aggressive they are, and how much risk they’re willing to take.
  • Letting players recover from their own shots but not from enemy hits is what kept them shooting instead of hiding.
  • A system like this only works if players can understand it quickly. When they can’t tell what’s going on, it feels unfair instead of tense.

Lumber Tycoon

Reward loops that announce themselves.

An incremental VR idle sim game about two kinds of reward. Bank enough timber and you can burn the whole run down for a permanent multiplier, or catch the power-up that drops in unannounced and vanishes if you’re not quick enough.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • For an idle game, it is numbers going up, which is not inherently satisfying. The reward has to be designed to create satisfaction and a feel of success.
  • Two types of rewards will feel the same if not distinguished carefully.
  • Unlike usual games, it is hard to make a popup to click in VR, so rewards are more interactive when players can physically reach for them.
  • For the game to be balanced, resource incrementation and the upgrading path should be carefully calculated, while preserving the interactive actions to obtain them.

Progression Systems

  • Prestige — bank enough elite resources and you can wipe the entire run for a permanent production multiplier that compounds every time you do it.
  • The Cost — all resources, generators, trophies and unlocks reset, making prestige a decision rather than a free bonus.
  • Surprise Surge — a power-up appears unannounced every minute or so and is gone if players don’t grab it in time. Catching it triples all production for a short window, rewarding attention rather than idling.

Feedback and Game Feel

  • Reusable Ease — pops objects in from nothing with a slight overshoot before settling, used for trophies and reward moments.
  • VFX — giving every interaction a visible response, so actions are reinforced by the feedback they earn.
  • Prestige — triggered with its own particle burst and sound, deliberately bigger than anything else in the game to showcase the ultimate achievement.
  • The Surge — has separate spawn and collection feedback, so players notice it arrive.
  • Live readout — counts maple toward the prestige threshold, so the goal is always legible.

Design Takeaway

  • Feedback intensity is a design parameter. Scaling the response to the rarity of the event tells the player which rewards are worth chasing.
  • Different feedback keeps components from blurring into one stream of idle numbers.
  • A reward players have to reach for in person is worth more than one players are simply told about.

The Haunted Hospital

You were not the first patient.

You wake on the floor of a hospital that was abandoned a long time ago. The doors are locked, gas is bleeding into the ward, and a voice tells you it wants to play a game. Search the lockers, cut open what was left behind, and find the way out before the air runs out.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • An escape room fails when it’s too legible and there’s no tension, or too ambiguous and the player stops believing a solution exists.
  • Horror narrows that window further, because fear already makes players reluctant to open the next door.
  • A countdown adds pressure but punishes hesitation, so the room has to keep confirming that progress is being made.

The Escape Loop

  • A scripted opening — gas filling the hospital, a clock running, and a way out to find. Then the game hands control to the player.
  • Locked gates — two keys, a knife and a battery. Keys are consumed, tools stay with you.
  • Socket rejection — the wrong item is pushed back out instead of accepted, so a bad guess costs seconds of the run.
  • Hidden items — items behind objects that have to be dealt with physically: an axe for the padlock, a blade for the body bag.
  • Red herrings — items that look exactly as important as the real ones and do nothing at all.
  • Collectibles — for players who explore instead of sprinting, counted on the end screen.
  • Full game flow — start screen, timed run, and separate win and loss endings with fades between them.

Atmosphere and Feedback

  • Dark by design — ambient light is switched off, so the only illumination is the fixtures placed in the room.
  • Guidance — low point lights in sickly green and cold blue do the guiding. It’s enough to show the way, but not enough to light the space, preserving the horror tone.
  • Objective completions — restoring power at the fuse box switches the lights on.
  • Physics — doors swing on their pivots with an invisible barrier that only lifts once the door has actually opened.

Design Takeaway

  • Misdirection is only fair when the wrong answer costs time rather than progress. A red herring that strands the player is a bug wearing a puzzle’s clothes.
  • Light is the cheapest feedback channel in VR. It confirms an objective without a word of UI.
  • Fear suppresses exploration, so a horror room has to reward poking around rather than assume it.

Infinite Matrix

The better you do, the faster it gets.

You’re flying down an endless digital tunnel, and it only gets faster the better you do. Weave through the gaps, shoot down whatever gets in your way, and grab a health potion when you can. One bad turn won’t end your run, but a few of them will.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • The starter was a simple tunnel flyer. The player steers through the gaps, and one hit ends the run. There wasn’t much to decide beyond steering.
  • I wanted to turn it into a real mini game, with something to shoot, something to collect, and a reason to take risks.
  • It also had to get harder on its own the longer you survive, without ever stopping to load a new level.

What I Added

  • Health — a health bar instead of instant death, so a mistake costs you but doesn’t end the run.
  • Shooting — fire projectiles down the tunnel to clear enemies before you reach them.
  • Enemies — they show up inside tunnel sections. Shoot them for points, or take damage if you fly into them.
  • Health Potions — pickups that restore health, so players can recover from earlier mistakes.
  • Shield — health potions also add a shield, an extra layer of protection that soaks up damage before your health does.
  • Spawn Chances — every new tunnel section rolls whether to place enemies and potions, so randomization is introduced to increase replayability.
  • Rotating Walls — the walls spin, so the gap you’re aiming for keeps moving.

Pacing and Feedback

  • Speed Ramp — your forward speed climbs with your score, up to a cap, so the game gets harder the better you do.
  • Score — points for every wall you pass and every enemy you shoot.
  • HUD — a health bar, shield count and score that update as you play.

Design Takeaway

  • A runner gets interesting once the player has choices. Shooting, pickups and damage turned steering into decision-making.
  • Tying difficulty to score keeps good players challenged without punishing new ones.
  • A little randomness, like spawn chances and moving walls, goes a long way in a game built from the same tunnel over and over.

  • Players can’t learn three enemies at once. Bringing them in a step at a time made the arena feel fair instead of chaotic.
  • The quiet climb after the arena matters as much as the arena itself. Players need a breather before the ending lands.
  • A clear goal at the top makes a vertical level feel like progress, not just getting higher.

S.01 / FORECASTINGSolo Project

ParkCast

Will there be a space when you get there?

Taipei’s live parking counts are minutes old by the time you read them, and in the daytime nearly half the city changes in between. ParkCast learns each car park’s rhythm from millions of readings and forecasts the chance of a space when you actually arrive, then ranks your options by what the whole trip is likely to cost.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • The official feed is late by design. It publishes every five minutes, about three minutes behind, and 40–47% of car parks change between readings in the daytime. A “live” number is already history.
  • A driver twenty minutes out doesn’t need the count right now. They need the odds at arrival, weighed against the walk and the risk of being turned away.
  • Built solo and meant to stay free, so nothing could cost money per user or fall over under load.

What I Built

  • Data Collector — reads the city feed about twenty seconds after each update, keeps two days hot in SQLite, and folds every finished day into compressed Parquet. It has since grown to six cities’ feeds.
  • Trip-Cost Ranking — scores each car park by the expected cost of the whole trip, so the closest lot at 21% loses to one a minute farther that almost certainly has room. Drivers can lean it toward cheaper or closer.
  • Map App — an installable, map-first React app with offline place search down to the lane, built from self-hosted map tiles with no API keys. Each car park also shows its typical week as a heat-map.
  • Honest Data — a car park whose sensor stops moving shows “not updating” instead of a fake 0% or 100%, because a stale certainty is worse than no answer.
  • Free-Tier Deployment — the whole app reads two small static files from Cloudflare Workers, refreshed every five minutes by a locked-down collector container.

How the Prediction Works

  • Framing — forecasts a probability, not a count, for the moment you arrive. The horizon is measured from the reading’s own timestamp, so the feed’s lag is corrected rather than ignored.
  • Patterns from History — learns each car park’s chance of a space for every half-hour of the week. Where a slot has little history, it leans on the lot’s overall rate, then the city’s. Public holidays come from the official calendar and get their own pattern, falling back to Sunday, which measured closest.
  • Blending with Now — the live reading dominates the next few minutes and fades on a 30-minute half-life as the arrival gets further away. Forecasts are precomputed for over a thousand car parks at 24 horizons every five minutes, plus a weekly table for arrivals up to seven days out.
  • Machine Learning Model — a LightGBM model built on 26 features covering the car park, the time of week, the live reading and the horizon. It retrains nightly from scratch, and a gate scores every new fit against the blend on a held-out day.
  • Training Fix — found that every training row was missing the live reading that every real prediction has. Fixing that mismatch cut the model’s error by 20%, and an ablation study then measured which features earned their place.
  • Evaluation — a leak-free, walk-forward backtest scores exactly what the app would publish. It reports the car parks that actually fill up, and calibration, separately, so easy cases can’t flatter the result.

Design Takeaway

  • The question shapes the product. “How many spaces now?” is a lookup; “will there be one when I get there?” is a forecast with a decision on top.
  • Training and serving have to see the same world. The biggest gain came from fixing what the model was trained on, not from making it fancier.
  • Saying “I don’t know” is a feature. A grey “not updating” beats a confident wrong answer.
S.02 / INDUSTRYSYSTEX · Gemini Data

Custom LLM Platform

Bring your own model, keep every call in check.

A data analytics platform where enterprise customers bring their own AI models. Admins connect providers like Azure, Anthropic or Vertex AI, every project picks the model it answers with, and a central gateway decides who may call what and keeps track of what each call costs.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • Customers want to use their own AI endpoints. At the same time, no one should ever see others’ API keys, and every call has to be charged to the right project.
  • Providers have different settings, and a model that works for chat may not work for others like embedding. The platform has to adapt to the different providers to ensure functionality.
  • As more company apps started using AI, every call needed one place to decide who’s allowed, without slowing down streamed replies.

Custom LLM Settings

  • Providers and Credentials — admins turn providers on, and workspace owners add their API keys. Secrets stay on the server and are never sent back to the browser.
  • Enterprise Gateways — a large enterprise customer runs its own AI gateway, which needed a non-standard auth header and refused streamed replies. Added a custom auth header option and an automatic fallback to non-streamed calls, after reproducing the failure against a fake copy of their gateway.
  • Models — each workspace sets up its own chat, embedding and Responses API models, and picks a default.
  • Advanced Settings — temperature, top-k, max tokens, reasoning effort and more. The form checks which ones each model actually supports, flags the rest, and warns before saving, so an unsupported setting never breaks chat.
  • Per-Project Models — every project picks its own model and gets its own locked-down key, with defaults falling back from project to workspace to the whole system.
  • Chat Routing — the platform’s chatbot answers with each project’s model, with caching and a safe fallback so chat never breaks.
  • Chat and Embedding Models — projects pick separate models for chat and document search, and the embedding model locks once documents are indexed.
  • Web Grounding — a per-model switch that lets Google’s models search the web while answering, with a list of domains to leave out.
  • Usage Report — token usage broken down by project and model, traced back from the gateway’s logs.

LLM Access Gateway

  • Research — compared ways to connect the company’s login system to the LLM gateway, checking every claim against the actual source code.
  • Design — went through several designs. The final one puts a check in front of the gateway, so apps never hold an API key and streamed replies never pass through our service.
  • Access Checks — every call is checked against the user’s login, the app, and what their company has access to. Each user gets their own key, swapped in on the fly.
  • Proof Package — built and tested the whole setup locally: automated checks, including a deliberately broken config to prove the tests can fail.
  • Management API — designed and built the API for providers, credentials and models in the production codebase.

Design Takeaway

  • Don’t trust the docs alone. Several things the gateway’s documentation promised turned out wrong, so every design decision was checked against the real source or a live test first.
  • Keeping our own records instead of reading them back from the gateway removed a whole class of stale-data bugs.
  • Security that relies on every app behaving isn’t security. Apps never hold a key, so there’s nothing for them to leak.

Auditable Energy Reporting

One dataset. Traceable reporting.

Companies buying renewable energy certificates still build their compliance filings by hand, pulling records from registries that each store data their own way. For Innovo Markets, a REC marketplace, I led the systems and software design of a reporting feature that turns those scattered records into audit-ready PDF and CSV reports from one dataset.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • No two registries agree. One is organized around power plants, the other around the certificates themselves, so the same fact lives in different places.
  • Every regulator wants its own shape. Illinois alone has two agencies with separate filings, and voluntary standards like RE100 ask for something else again.
  • A certificate can only be claimed once. A report that counts the same REC twice is worse than no report at all.

What I Built

  • Requirements Map — traced the reporting rules for nine state markets plus RE100 and the GHG Protocol down to the exact platform fields that satisfy them, as rule diagrams in FigJam.
  • Registry Analysis — took both registries apart field by field to learn what each filing needs and where that data actually lives.
  • Universal Evidence Model — a single record format every report is built from, carrying retirement details, serial ranges, vintage and a fingerprint of the registry’s proof of retirement.
  • Integrity Checks — designed checks that confirm a certificate was really retired, that its quantity matches its serial range, and that it qualifies for the program being reported.
  • Report Generator — a Python prototype that fills HTML templates and prints compliance and voluntary reports to PDF, with matching CSV exports from the same data.
  • Reporting UI — an interactive prototype of a new Reports page. Pick a state or standard and a period, and download the report. The mockups also covered reporting by folder or by hand-picked certificates.
  • Hand-off — a technical spec for Innovo’s engineers covering the script, the data maps, and how to swap mock data for live queries.

Business Case

  • Pricing Simulation — built a Monte Carlo model that runs 1,000 scenarios at each upfront price and tracks the client’s NPV and IRR, showing where the feature stops paying for itself.
  • Customer Savings — paired with the team’s five-year savings model, the case came out NPV-positive for both small and large clients.
  • Rollout — recommended a phased launch: priority states first, a pilot with a few customers, then the full rollout.

Design Takeaway

  • Most of the work happened before any code. Once every requirement pointed at a real field, the generator was the easy part.
  • Auditability has to live in the data itself, not get bolted onto the report at the end.
  • Putting a price on the feature changed the conversation. The simulation turned “customers will save time” into a range Innovo could actually charge.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • Research papers aren’t built to be searched by section. Every journal styles its headings differently, and the text is cluttered with author lists, equations, citations and running headers.
  • Exact keywords and paraphrases pull in opposite directions. Word matching misses a question asked differently, while meaning matching can drift away from a precise technical term.
  • “It feels better” doesn’t count. Every change needed a number behind it.

What I Built

  • Reading the PDFs — pulls every line along with its font, size and weight, and cleans up the usual extraction mess like words running together.
  • Finding the Sections — learns which fonts are body text and which are headings in each paper, then filters out bylines, formulas and page headers before cutting it into titled sections with page ranges.
  • Keyword Search — a BM25 index over every section, with stemming and a lower weight for reference lists so they don’t crowd the top.
  • Meaning Search — embeds each section with Sentence-BERT, splitting long ones into chunks first so nothing gets cut off.
  • Hybrid Ranking — one adjustable weight blends both scores into a single ranking.
  • App Backend — turns the offline pipeline into a live app. The index and model load once and stay in memory, saved embeddings are reused unless the sections or model change, and every search mode hands back results in one common format.
  • Search App — a Streamlit front end where you pick a mode, drag the blend weight, and read each hit with its paper, page range, score breakdown and highlighted snippet.

Measuring It

  • Benchmark — a labeled set of test questions mixing exact terms, topics and paraphrases, with every answer section graded for relevance. The team later grew it to 30.
  • Evaluation — runs each mode through precision, recall, MRR and nDCG, then sweeps the blend from all-meaning to all-keyword to pick the best weight.
  • Results — on the final 31 papers (1,184 sections), the hybrid beat both methods alone: nDCG@10 of 0.49, against 0.41 for keywords and 0.47 for meaning. The winning blend leaned 80% on meaning.

Design Takeaway

  • Search quality is decided before the first query. Miss a heading, and no ranking formula can bring that section back.
  • Neither signal wins alone. Keywords anchor technical terms, and meaning catches the way people actually ask.
  • A small labeled benchmark turned tuning into a measurement, so the blend weight came from data, not a guess.

Self-Balancing Rod

Swing it up, catch it, hold it.

A motorized arm that flings a hanging rod up to vertical and then keeps it balanced there, correcting 200 times a second. I worked out the models and controllers in MATLAB and wrote the C that runs them on a TI microcontroller.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • An upside-down rod falls the instant you stop correcting it, and the only thing you can push is the arm at its base, never the rod itself.
  • The controller that balances the rod is useless while it hangs down, so it needed a second strategy to swing it up, and a handoff between the two that doesn’t drop it.
  • Real motors have friction and the encoders only report angles, so the math on paper never quite matches the hardware.

What I Built

  • Control Loop — a timer interrupt on the TI C2000 fires every 5 ms. It reads both encoders, keeps the rod angle within ±180° as it spins, computes the control and drives the motor’s PWM, all inside one tick.
  • Speed Estimates — the encoders only report angles, so each speed comes from a filtered derivative, discretized with the Tustin method into a two-term update the loop runs every tick.
  • Swing-Up — works out the rod’s energy each tick, from its motion and its height, and pushes the arm whichever way adds more. A cap on the effort keeps the motor in its comfortable range.
  • Catch and Handoff — switches to balancing only once the rod is within 0.3 rad (about 17°) of upright and the balance effort would stay reasonable. After the catch, the arm’s target walks back home in small steps so it doesn’t lurch, and an effort spike sends it back to swinging.
  • Friction Compensation — adds back the measured static and viscous friction, separately for each direction, so small corrections aren’t swallowed by stiction.

Calculations and Analysis

  • Pendulum Model — linearized the arm-and-rod dynamics into a four-state model, then discretized it at 5 ms with a zero-order hold so the design matches what the chip actually runs.
  • Pole Placement — chose closed-loop poles between −10 and −15 rad/s, mapped them into the discrete domain, and solved for the four feedback gains, keeping two candidate gain sets.
  • System Identification — fit a first-order motor model from test runs and converted it into the position model the controllers were designed on. Measured friction in both directions, and every later controller reused those numbers.
  • PID Design — tuned PI and PID loops on root-locus plots in MATLAB, converting between gains and the controller’s zero by hand. Added gain switching (a gentle PD while moving, a stiffer PID once settled) and anti-windup that freezes the integral when the motor saturates.
  • Flexible Beam — for a beam with almost no natural damping (ζ ≈ 0.005), averaged 16 ADC samples per reading and shaped every step command through a 6th-order filter so the beam wouldn’t ring. The filter was designed in MATLAB, checked for how its coefficients hold up in single precision, and run in extended precision on the chip.

Design Takeaway

  • The model gets you close, and the hardware decides the rest. Friction and noisy speed estimates were where the real tuning happened.
  • Two simple controllers with a careful handoff beat one controller trying to do everything.
  • Every equation ends up as a few lines that must finish inside a 5 ms window, so the math had to be written with the code in mind.

PROJECT NOTES

The Design Challenge

  • The car had to react to light with circuitry alone, with no processor deciding anything.
  • A light sensor produces only a small electrical change, while a motor needs real current, so the signal had to control something strong enough to drive the wheels.
  • Two motors and a tangle of wires share one breadboard, and a single bad connection can stop a wheel without any visible sign.

What I Built

  • Brightness Detector — a photoresistor and a 100 kΩ potentiometer form a voltage divider, so the room’s brightness sets the voltage and the potentiometer tunes where the car reacts. We measured the sensor from about 0.4 kΩ under a flashlight to 13.7 kΩ when covered, in line with the datasheet math.
  • Clean Switching — a CD40106 Schmitt trigger turns that slowly drifting voltage into a crisp on/off signal. Its two thresholds, about 3.6 V and 5.3 V, keep flickering light from making the motors stutter, and its output goes high in the dark.
  • Simulation First — modeled the trigger circuit in the Falstad simulator to find the exact switching points before wiring anything. We also checked datasheets for parts that would survive the 9 V supply; a common 74LS04 inverter tops out at 5.25 V.
  • Motor Driver — N-channel MOSFETs switch each motor from the ground side, with the Schmitt trigger’s output on the gate, so darkness means current through the wheels.
  • Breadboard Build — both circuits laid out on one breadboard on the chassis, running off a 9 V battery.
  • Debugging — when one motor wouldn’t spin, we ruled out wiring and power, narrowed it to the motor circuit, then measured the MOSFET’s gate and drain-to-source voltages under different light until the cause showed: its source wasn’t grounded. One wire fixed it.
  • Teaching Video — turned the story into a short video for future students, with hand-drawn diagrams and a MOSFET explained as a water valve.

Design Takeaway

  • Measure, don’t stare. Comparing voltages pin by pin found what looking at the wiring couldn’t.
  • A switch only works if the circuit closes, and the most basic connection is the easiest one to miss.
  • Explaining a fix to someone else is the best test of whether you really understood it.
